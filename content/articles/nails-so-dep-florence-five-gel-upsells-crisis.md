# The “Five Gel Upsells” Crisis: When a Regular Pedicure Escalates Into Bleeding, a Color Dispute, and “Get Out”

A customer walked into a nail salon asking for one of the simplest services on the menu:

**A regular pedicure.**

According to a customer review involving **Nails So Dep in Florence**, what followed was anything but simple.

She says she was asked approximately five times whether she wanted gel despite repeatedly requesting regular polish. She alleges that her cuticle was then cut badly enough that staff used clotting powder and cotton to control the bleeding. She further claims that her natural toenails were filed despite arriving without polish, that the selected polish did not resemble the display sample, and that she was eventually told the samples were approximately five years old.

The appointment then appears to have deteriorated into a payment confrontation.

According to the reviewer, after she indicated she was not going to pay, she was told to leave.

One online review cannot independently establish every detail of what occurred during the appointment.

But as a training case, the sequence illustrates how multiple individually recoverable problems can compound until neither the service nor the customer relationship can be saved.

## “Regular, Please” Should Not Require Five Answers

The first issue allegedly began before the pedicure became technically problematic.

The customer says she requested regular polish.

Then she was offered gel.

She declined.

According to her account, she was asked again — repeatedly, approximately five times.

Offering an upgrade is normal business.

Pressure is different.

A technician can reasonably explain:

“Gel is an additional $\_\_ and usually lasts longer. Would you like to upgrade?”

If the customer says no, the sales process should generally end.

**No should complete the upsell conversation.**

Repeatedly asking after receiving a clear answer can transform a recommendation into pressure.

More importantly, it communicates that the technician is not listening.

That perception becomes especially damaging if problems arise later in the appointment.

## Upselling Has a Point of Diminishing Returns

Salons understandably want to increase average ticket value.

Gel.

Spa upgrades.

Callus treatments.

Designs.

Wax.

Additional massage.

Premium products.

These can all legitimately increase revenue.

But successful upselling requires trust.

When a customer feels pressured, the additional $10 or $20 the salon hopes to generate can put the entire transaction at risk.

The objective should be:

**Inform once. Explain value. Respect the answer.**

A customer who feels respected may choose the upgrade next time.

A customer who feels pressured may choose another salon next time.

## The Pedicure Allegedly Became an Injury Incident

The complaint then becomes substantially more serious.

According to the reviewer, the technician cut around her toenail badly enough that the area began bleeding.

She says staff applied clotting powder and rubbed the area with cotton balls for approximately five minutes.

She describes the toenail area as visibly covered with blood.

The precise nature and severity of an injury cannot be determined from the review alone.

But once bleeding occurs during a service, the priority should immediately change.

This is no longer an upselling conversation.

It is no longer about appointment speed.

It is no longer about polish color.

The immediate priority is appropriate response to the incident, sanitation, and the customer's wellbeing.

## When Bleeding Occurs, the Normal Service Flow Should Stop

One of the worst operational habits in beauty services is treating an injury like a minor interruption that simply needs to be handled quickly so the appointment can continue.

A better principle is:

**Stop. Assess. Follow appropriate sanitation and incident procedures. Then determine whether the service should continue.**

Management should also document what occurred according to applicable salon procedures.

The customer needs to see that the incident is being taken seriously.

Even an accidental injury can sometimes be recovered as a customer-service situation when the business responds calmly, professionally, and responsibly.

A dismissive or rushed response can make the incident substantially worse.

## “There Was Nothing on My Toenails”

The reviewer raises another technical concern.

She says she arrived without toenail polish but believed the technician nevertheless ground or filed away part of her natural nail.

There may be legitimate preparation steps involving careful surface work depending on the service being performed.

But the customer clearly did not understand why this was happening.

That creates another training problem:

**Never perform a step the customer perceives as destructive without being able to explain why it is necessary.**

If preparation is required, explain it.

If only minimal surface preparation is appropriate, use appropriate technique.

And if the customer asks why something is being done, stop long enough to answer.

The customer should never leave believing:

**“They removed part of my natural nail for absolutely no reason.”**

## Then the Color Did Not Match

After the physical complaint came an aesthetic one.

The reviewer says the polish applied to her toes did not resemble the color she selected from the salon's sample.

Color variation can occur for legitimate reasons.

Display tips can age.

Pigments can change.

Different numbers of coats can affect appearance.

Lighting can change perception.

Manufacturers can modify formulas.

But the salon owns the sample being presented to customers.

If a display no longer represents the actual product, it should not continue functioning as the customer's purchasing reference.

## “The Samples Were Five Years Old”

According to the reviewer, the owner explained the mismatch by saying the samples were approximately **five years old**.

From a customer-service perspective, that explanation creates a new problem instead of solving the existing one.

The customer did not create the outdated display.

The salon did.

If management knows a sample has aged enough that it no longer accurately represents the polish, continuing to show that sample transfers an internal maintenance problem onto customers.

The appropriate response is not:

“We can't help that the sample doesn't match.”

The appropriate operational response is:

**Replace inaccurate samples.**

A color chart is effectively part of the sales process.

Customers use it to decide what they are purchasing.

It should therefore reasonably represent what will actually be applied.

## The Polish Had to Come Off

The reviewer says she ultimately asked staff to remove the polish because she was so unhappy with the mismatch.

Think about how far the appointment had deteriorated.

She entered wanting a regular pedicure.

She allegedly endured repeated gel offers.

Then came the bleeding incident.

Then concern about her natural nails being filed.

Then the polish color did not meet her expectation.

Then the polish was removed.

At this point, the service had almost completely reversed itself.

The customer entered expecting to leave with finished toes.

Instead, according to her account, she was moving backward toward the condition in which she arrived.

## Then Came the Payment Conflict

The review says the customer was not going to pay.

Whether a customer is legally or contractually entitled to refuse payment in a particular situation depends on facts and applicable rules that cannot be determined from a review.

But from a customer-service training perspective, management should recognize when a transaction has moved beyond an ordinary dissatisfaction complaint.

This customer was allegedly describing an injury, an unwanted or misunderstood technical step, an inaccurate color result, and removal of the polish.

That is a situation requiring management intervention.

Instead, according to the customer, the dispute escalated further.

## “I Know — You Get Out”

The reviewer claims she was eventually told to leave, recounting language along the lines of:

**“I know. You get out.”**

If accurately reported, that becomes the final defining moment of the experience.

Until then, management still had some opportunity to de-escalate.

They could inspect the service.

Listen.

Clarify what happened.

Discuss the bill.

Document the injury.

Explain what could or could not reasonably be corrected.

Once the conversation becomes “get out,” recovery is essentially over.

The salon is no longer solving a complaint.

It is ending the relationship.

## The Customer Was Already Leaving Anyway

There is an important psychological point here.

The reviewer essentially responds:

Why tell me to leave when I would never return after this experience anyway?

That demonstrates how unnecessary hostility can add reputational damage without producing any business benefit.

The customer was already dissatisfied.

The salon had already likely lost the repeat business.

A hostile final interaction only gives the customer another story to tell publicly.

Sometimes management cannot save the customer.

But management can still control how professionally the relationship ends.

## One Failure Made the Next Failure Worse

This case is particularly useful for training because no incident occurred in isolation.

Imagine the color mismatch happening during an otherwise excellent pedicure.

The customer might simply select another color.

Imagine one additional gel recommendation during a friendly appointment.

Probably insignificant.

Imagine a small disagreement about a preparation step when the technician has already established trust.

Potentially easy to explain.

But according to this reviewer, these events accumulated.

**Repeated upselling → alleged injury → concern about natural-nail filing → color mismatch → outdated-sample explanation → polish removal → payment dispute → ejection.**

Each event reduced the customer's remaining patience for the next one.

That is how service failures compound.

## Management Perspective: Recognize the Escalation Point

Salon managers need to know when a technician should stop trying to handle a situation alone.

A complaint involving bleeding should be one obvious escalation point.

A customer asking for the completed polish to be removed should be another.

A dispute over whether the service should be paid for definitely requires management attention.

At those moments, the goal should not be defending every decision the salon made.

The goal is to understand:

What happened?

What does the customer believe happened?

What can still be corrected?

What does salon policy require?

And how can the situation end professionally even if the customer never returns?

## Media & Customer Experience Perspective

The reputational danger of this review comes from its escalation.

The customer says she wanted a regular pedicure.

She allegedly had to decline gel repeatedly.

She says she was cut badly enough that staff needed several minutes to control bleeding.

She believed unnecessary filing was performed on her natural nails.

She disliked the polish color.

She says management attributed the mismatch to five-year-old samples.

The polish was removed.

A payment dispute followed.

And according to her account, she was ultimately told to get out.

The individual allegations cannot be independently established from one customer's review.

But as a business case study, the lesson is clear:

**A salon cannot always prevent the first mistake. It can prevent the first mistake from becoming seven mistakes.**

An accidental cut does not require an argument.

A color mismatch does not require hostility.

A declined upsell does not need to be offered five more times.

A complaint does not need to become a confrontation.

And a customer who is already unhappy does not need to leave feeling humiliated as well.

The original appointment was for something extraordinarily ordinary:

**A regular pedicure.**

According to this reviewer, the salon transformed it into an experience she described as the worst service she had ever received.

That is the management lesson.

The biggest crises often do not begin as crises.

They become crises because nobody recognizes when it is time to stop selling, stop defending, start listening, and take control of the customer experience.
